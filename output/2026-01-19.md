# Research Summary: Learning SDE Models of Tropical Cyclone Intensification

**Main Contribution:**
Demonstrates that interpretable, physics-based differential equation models of tropical cyclone intensification can be automatically learned from data rather than hand-derived. Presents a 10-term cubic stochastic differential equation (SDE) that captures realistic intensification dynamics while remaining analytically tractable.

**Method:**
- System identification to learn SDE coefficients from observational data
- Model structure: cubic polynomial SDE with environmental feature inputs
- Training via maximum likelihood estimation on intensity time series
- Validation through synthetic ensemble generation and statistical comparison

**Data:**
- IBTrACS hurricane intensity records (Northern Hemisphere)
- ERA5 reanalysis for large-scale environmental variables
- Engineered features: established environmental predictors of intensification (e.g., sea surface temperature, wind shear, relative humidity)

**Climate Relevance:**
Addresses critical gap in tropical cyclone hazard quantification by enabling physics-informed synthetic storm generation. Improves risk assessment for climate adaptation and emergency preparedness despite limited historical hurricane records.

**Implications:**
- Enables large ensemble simulations for probabilistic hazard estimation
- Provides interpretable alternative to black-box ML models for operational forecasting
- Framework applicable to other complex earth system dynamics requiring data-driven mechanistic models

**Limitations:**
- Restricted to Northern Hemisphere; generalization to other basins unclear
- Dependent on ERA5 reanalysis quality and feature engineering choices
- Limited discussion of model skill relative to existing physics-based and statistical approaches

**TL;DR:**
Automated system identification learns a simple, interpretable stochastic differential equation model of hurricane intensification that generates realistic synthetic storms for hazard assessment.

# WetSAM: Wetland Mapping from Sparse Annotations

**Main Contribution:**
Proposes WetSAM, a temporal-aware adaptation of the Segment Anything Model (SAM) that enables accurate wetland mapping from sparse point labels by integrating satellite time series data. Addresses the critical gap between foundation models' static-image design and the dynamic nature of wetland ecosystems.

**Method:**
- Dual-branch architecture: (1) temporally prompted branch extends SAM with hierarchical adapters and dynamic temporal aggregation to capture phenological variability; (2) spatial branch uses temporally constrained region-growing to generate pseudo-labels
- Bidirectional consistency regularization jointly optimizes both branches
- Leverages multi-temporal satellite imagery to disentangle wetland characteristics from seasonal dynamics

**Data:**
Eight global wetland regions (~5,000 km² each); sparse point-level annotations used as supervision signals; satellite image time series (specific sensors/platforms not detailed in abstract).

**Climate Relevance:**
Wetlands are critical carbon sinks and biodiversity hotspots essential for climate mitigation and ecosystem resilience monitoring. Accurate, scalable mapping supports climate adaptation strategies and wetland conservation under changing environmental conditions.

**Implications:**
Enables cost-effective, high-resolution wetland monitoring at global scale with minimal manual labeling; supports ecosystem monitoring, carbon accounting, and climate policy implementation across diverse geographic regions.

**Limitations:**
Evaluation limited to eight regions; generalization to extreme wetland types or data-scarce regions unclear; computational requirements for time series processing not discussed.

# Research Summary: Geometry of Aggregate Snowflakes

**Main Contribution:**
Develops a statistical parameterization of snowflake aggregate geometry based on physically-based modeling, capturing the natural variability in snowflake properties that current climate models ignore.

**Method:**
- Physics-based aggregation model simulating collision and coalescence of primary ice crystals
- Generated millions of synthetic snowflakes to derive statistical distributions
- Characterized geometric properties: maximum dimension, aspect ratio, cross-sectional area, and their correlations
- Fitted lognormal distributions to relate aggregate size to geometric parameters

**Data:**
Synthetic snowflake dataset generated from aggregation model; validated against observed Doppler radar spectra and precipitation observations.

**Climate Relevance:**
Snowflakes are critical precipitation components in weather and climate systems—a substantial fraction of surface precipitation originates as ice. Realistic representation of snowflake properties directly affects precipitation rates, cloud microphysics, and radiative transfer in climate models.

**Implications:**
- Enables stochastic representation of snowflake variability in Lagrangian particle models
- Improves simulated fall velocities and aggregation growth rates
- Produces Doppler radar spectra closer to observations, enhancing model-observation agreement for validation and data assimilation

**Limitations:**
Apparent focus on aggregate properties; unclear how parameterization performs across different atmospheric conditions or crystal habit distributions in real clouds.

**TL;DR:**
Statistical parameterization of snowflake geometry from synthetic aggregates enables realistic stochastic representation of precipitation in climate models.

# Research Summary: Carbon Tax Distributional Impact Modelling

**Main Contribution:**
Systematically reviews methodological choices in microsimulation models used to assess carbon tax impacts across population groups, and meta-analyzes how these choices influence regressivity findings across 217 estimates from 71 countries.

**Method:**
Literature review of microsimulation modelling approaches combined with probit regression meta-analysis to quantify how specific modelling choices (tax design, emission coverage, data sources, behavioral assumptions, general equilibrium effects) predict the likelihood of finding regressive distributional outcomes.

**Data:**
217 distributional impact estimates extracted from 71 countries; meta-analysis examines variation in underlying datasets, model specifications, and outcome metrics across published studies.

**Climate Relevance:**
Carbon taxation is a primary policy instrument for emissions reduction, but political viability depends on perceived fairness; understanding distributional impacts is essential for designing equitable climate policy that maintains public support.

**Implications:**
- Carbon border adjustment mechanisms may reduce regressivity by including imported emissions
- Behavioral responses and income-adjusted metrics lower regressivity findings
- General equilibrium effects increase regressivity likelihood, suggesting economy-wide interactions matter
- No standardized modelling approach exists, complicating policy comparisons across jurisdictions

**Limitations:**
Meta-analysis captures published estimates only (potential publication bias); heterogeneity in tax designs and national contexts limits generalizability of findings to specific policy contexts.

**TL;DR:**
Methodological choices in carbon tax microsimulation models substantially affect regressivity conclusions, with emission coverage scope and behavioral assumptions being particularly influential.

# CROCS: Smart Meter Consumer Segmentation Summary

**Method:**
- Stage 1: Individual clustering of daily load profiles per consumer → Representative Load Sets (RLS)
- Stage 2: Consumer-level clustering using Weighted Sum of Minimum Distances (WSMD), a novel set-to-set distance metric

**Data:**
Synthetic and real Australian smart meter datasets; experiments validate performance on large-scale deployments.

**Climate Relevance:**

**Implications:**

**Limitations:**
Paper abstract does not detail computational complexity comparisons, scalability thresholds, or validation against domain-expert segmentations; generalizability beyond Australian context unclear.
