Demonstrates a deep learning framework that translates dynamical circulation forecasts into interpretable regional precipitation predictions, successfully forecasting a 2026 summer drought over central China with physically validated mechanisms.

**Method:**

Enables evidence-based seasonal drought warnings months in advance; provides stakeholders with physically grounded explanations for AI predictions rather than black-box forecasts; methodology transferable to other regions and precipitation anomalies.

**Limitations:**
Skill assessment based on analogue years rather than independent test set; single-region focus; requires validation as 2026 approaches; dependence on quality of underlying dynamical circulation predictions.

**TL;DR:**
An interpretable deep learning model predicts a 2026 summer drought in central China by translating circulation forecasts into precipitation estimates, with LRP-validated attribution to anomalous northerly winds driven by equatorial Pacific warming.

# Research Summary: Extremes on Rewind

**Main Contribution:**

**Method:**

Three historical extreme weather events: 2021 Pacific Northwest heatwave, Superstorm Sandy, and Hurricane Ian; validated against reanalysis data.

**Climate Relevance:**
Addresses critical challenge in extreme event attribution and scenario planning—efficiently sampling the space of possible precursor conditions and trajectories leading to high-impact weather, essential for understanding event likelihood and risk assessment.

**Implications:**
Enables rapid generation of physically plausible alternative scenarios for rare extremes, improving hazard planning by revealing diverse pathways to extreme outcomes (e.g., varying hurricane intensities, landfall locations, antecedent heat patterns).

**Limitations:**
End-conditioned ensembles show systematic biases (e.g., PNW heatwave members persistently warmer than observed); validation limited to three case studies; computational cost and model skill not explicitly quantified.

**TL;DR:**
Non-autoregressive AI models can efficiently generate large ensembles of extreme weather scenarios by conditioning on final states, revealing diverse atmospheric pathways without exhaustive data searching.

**Published:** 2026-08-14
**Categories:** physics.ao-ph, physics.comp-ph
**Relevance Score:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐

# Research Summary: Wasserstein Distance for Climate Variability Quantification

**Main Contribution:**
Introduces a novel metric using 1-Wasserstein distance (optimal transport) to quantify the relative magnitude of internal variability in single-model initial-condition large ensembles (SMILEs). The metric provides a distribution-agnostic alternative to existing variance-based approaches.

**Method:**
Computes two 1-Wasserstein distances measuring forced and internal variability separately, then calculates their ratio. The approach requires only sorting and quantile differencing with no tunable parameters, making it computationally efficient and applicable to non-Gaussian distributions.

**Data:**
Validated on synthetic data (Gaussian, uniform, lognormal distributions) and applied to Community Earth System Model Large Ensemble (CESM-LE) outputs: 2m air temperature and total precipitation under two forcing scenarios.

**Climate Relevance:**
Directly addresses a critical challenge in climate science: separating anthropogenic climate signals from natural internal variability. Accurate quantification is essential for robust detection of forced climate change and reliable uncertainty assessment in projections.

**Implications:**
Enables more robust analysis of large-ensemble climate datasets with improved stability across variables and regions. Facilitates clearer attribution of climate change signals and better characterization of uncertainty ranges in climate predictions.

**Limitations:**
Requires ensemble size ≥40 members for stable estimates; applicability to other climate models and variables beyond those tested remains to be demonstrated.

**TL;DR:**
Wasserstein distance provides a parameter-free, distribution-robust metric for quantifying internal variability in climate ensembles, outperforming existing methods across diverse data types.

# Research Summary: SST-Prescribed Simulations and Earth's Feedback Parameter

**Main Contribution:**
Demonstrates that the standard prescribed sea-surface temperature (SST) method for estimating Earth's climate feedback parameter is fundamentally limited by missing atmospheric forcing and statistical noise, making it unreliable for detecting temporal changes in feedback over observational timescales.

**Method:**
• Controlled experiments comparing fully-coupled historical simulations with prescribed-SST atmospheric model runs
• Moving-window regression analysis to detect feedback parameter trends
• 4000-year pre-industrial control simulation to establish noise baselines
• Statistical significance testing against null hypothesis of no SST-feedback relationship

**Data:**
Coupled climate model simulations (historical and pre-industrial control); observed SST patterns; atmospheric forcing fields.

**Climate Relevance:**
Earth's feedback parameter directly determines climate sensitivity—the warming response to doubled CO₂. Accurate feedback estimation is essential for constraining climate projections and understanding how ocean patterns influence global temperature response to forcing.

**Implications:**
Prescribed-SST experiments cannot reliably attribute feedback changes to evolving SST patterns on decadal-to-centennial scales. Alternative methods (e.g., fully-coupled simulations, longer observational windows) are needed to robustly estimate feedback evolution and improve climate sensitivity constraints.

**Limitations:**
Analysis limited to model-based SST prescriptions; findings may not directly apply to observational SST datasets; pre-industrial control may not fully represent modern forcing complexity.

**TL;DR:**
Prescribed-SST simulations cannot reliably detect feedback changes because missing atmospheric forcing and statistical noise obscure any genuine SST-feedback relationships on observational timescales.
