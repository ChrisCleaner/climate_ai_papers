# Research Summary: Sea-Level Change from U.S. Vehicle Emissions

**Main Contribution:**
Extends EPA climate modeling to quantify long-term sea-level rise (SLR) impacts from U.S. vehicle emissions using probabilistic uncertainty quantification. Demonstrates that transportation sector emissions reductions yield substantially larger benefits over multi-century timescales than previously estimated by EPA analyses.

**Method:**
Coupled FaIR climate model (emissions-to-temperature) with BRICK sea-level model (temperature-to-SLR), applying probabilistic weighting to represent joint climate-sea-level uncertainty. Extended modeling horizon beyond 2100 to capture long-term commitment effects.

**Data:**
EPA vehicle emissions scenarios; SSP2-4.5 baseline scenario; counterfactual pre-regulation fleet data; U.S. coastal downscaling regions (emphasis on Gulf of Mexico).

**Climate Relevance:**
Quantifies the often-overlooked contribution of transportation emissions to GMSLR and regional coastal impacts. Demonstrates path-dependency of climate commitments—emissions reductions today prevent amplified SLR decades to centuries later through thermal inertia and ice-sheet dynamics.

**Implications:**
Supports policy case for vehicle emissions standards by revealing multi-century SLR benefits (>6 cm by 2200). Highlights disproportionate coastal vulnerability in specific U.S. regions, informing adaptation planning and cost-benefit analyses of transportation decarbonization.

**Limitations:**
Relies on model-dependent uncertainty representations; downscaling methodology not detailed; counterfactual scenarios are speculative; does not address socioeconomic adaptation capacity.

**TL;DR:**
U.S. vehicle emissions reductions prevent 6+ cm of regional sea-level rise by 2200, with amplified effects along Gulf coasts.

# Research Summary: Extreme Precipitation Return Time Forecasting

**Main Contribution:**
Develops a large deviation theory framework using the Landau distribution to accurately forecast return times of extreme precipitation events globally, enabling predictions beyond historical observations across different climate scenarios.

**Method:**
- Applies Landau distribution (from plasma physics) to model extreme precipitation tail behavior
- Fits distribution to observational data to estimate large deviation rate functions
- Maps historical return time relationships to CMIP6 climate projections under multiple emission scenarios
- Validates against conventional extreme value distributions (Gumbel, Weibull, Generalized Extreme Value)

**Data:**
Global precipitation observations; CMIP6 multi-model ensemble projections under various Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs)

**Climate Relevance:**
Addresses critical gap in extreme weather prediction by quantifying how precipitation intensity thresholds that were rare historically will become more frequent under future warming, with direct implications for infrastructure and disaster planning.

**Implications:**
Enables probabilistic forecasting of extreme precipitation beyond instrumental records; reveals that 21st-century cohorts face substantially elevated lifetime exposure to extreme precipitation under most emission scenarios, informing climate adaptation policy and risk assessment.

**Limitations:**
Success rate of 93% for Landau distribution suggests regional variability; reliance on CMIP6 projections inherits model uncertainties; return time extrapolation assumes distributional stability under climate change.

**TL;DR:**
Large deviation theory with Landau distributions accurately predicts extreme precipitation return times globally and projects sharply increased exposure for future generations under warming scenarios.

# Research Summary: Energy Use Drivers in the U.S.

**Main Contribution:**
Quantifies the relative importance of prices, efficiency policies, GDP, and climate in explaining cross-state energy consumption differences. Demonstrates that intensity improvements—not reduced demand—drove the 12.8% per capita energy decline from 2006–2022.

**Method:**
• LMDI (Logarithmic Mean Divisia Index) decomposition: isolates intensity, activity, and structural effects
• Stochastic frontier analysis: separates "demand frontier" (efficient consumption) from inefficiency above it
• Variable-importance methods: attributes variance in energy use to specific drivers

**Data:**
Panel dataset of 50 U.S. states plus DC, 2006–2022; includes energy consumption, prices, GDP, climate variables, and state-level efficiency policy indicators.

**Climate Relevance:**
Identifies policy levers most effective for reducing energy-related emissions. Shows efficiency regulations and pricing mechanisms outweigh climate/geography factors in determining consumption patterns, informing decarbonization strategy design.

**Implications:**
Policy makers should prioritize energy pricing mechanisms and efficiency standards as primary tools for reducing consumption. Results suggest regulatory approaches are more impactful than relying on economic growth or climate adaptation alone.

**Limitations:**
Analysis limited to U.S. states; cross-national applicability unclear. Causality between policies and outcomes inferred from variance decomposition rather than causal identification. Efficiency policy measurement relies on state-level indicators that may not capture implementation variation.

**TL;DR:**
Energy pricing and efficiency policies drive 39% of cross-state U.S. energy consumption differences, far exceeding climate and GDP factors.
