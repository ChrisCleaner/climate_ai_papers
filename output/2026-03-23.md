# Research Summary: Wind and Solar Uncertainty in Climate Projections

**Main Contribution:**
Quantifies how regional climate models (RCMs) and global climate models (GCMs) contribute differently to uncertainty in wind and solar resource projections. Demonstrates that uncertainty sources vary by variable, region, and season—critical for reliable renewable energy planning under climate change.

**Method:**
Variance decomposition analysis applied to 31 RCM-GCM model pairs, isolating contributions from each model type across spatial and seasonal dimensions. Separates absolute historical field variability from projected change uncertainty.

**Data:**
31 regional-global climate model combinations; spatially and seasonally resolved wind speed and solar radiation fields (historical and projected).

**Climate Relevance:**
Wind and solar resources are climate-sensitive variables essential for assessing renewable energy viability under future climate scenarios. Uncertainty quantification is critical for climate adaptation in the energy sector.

**Implications:**
Guides strategic ensemble design for climate projections supporting energy system planning. Suggests prioritizing GCM improvements for wind change projections and RCM improvements for summer solar projections; highlights mountainous regions requiring special attention.

**Limitations:**
Analysis limited to 31 model pairs; findings may not generalize to newer model generations or alternative ensemble designs. Doesn't address model biases or skill in reproducing observed variability.

**TL;DR:**
GCMs and RCMs drive uncertainty differently in wind/solar projections depending on variable, season, and topography—essential knowledge for designing climate ensembles for energy decisions.

# Research Summary: Transition Path Theory for Hurricane Rapid Intensification

**Main Contribution:**
Applies transition path theory (TPT) to rigorously quantify how upper ocean conditions—particularly barrier layer thickness and thermocline depth—influence hurricane rapid intensification (RI) in the tropical Atlantic. Reveals that thermocline depth is a stronger RI predictor than barrier layer presence alone, with translational speed as a critical modulating factor.

**Method:**
Constructs Markov chains from discretized time series of hurricane intensity, intensity change, translational speed, and oceanographic variables. Uses TPT statistics to compute first-passage time distributions from source states (e.g., specific barrier layer/intensity combinations) to RI target states, conditioning on minimal-detour pathways to isolate causal relationships.

**Data:**
Hurricane and ocean reanalysis datasets covering the eastern Caribbean Sea and western tropical North Atlantic, with emphasis on barrier layer thickness and thermocline depth measurements.

**Climate Relevance:**
Addresses rapid intensification—a critical climate adaptation challenge as RI events cause disproportionate damage and are difficult to forecast. Links freshwater input (river runoff) to ocean stratification and RI susceptibility, connecting terrestrial hydrology to tropical cyclone behavior.

**Implications:**
Improved RI prediction by identifying thermocline depth as a key oceanographic precursor; supports enhanced early warning systems and climate risk assessment for Atlantic hurricane-prone regions.

**Limitations:**
Regional focus (eastern Caribbean/western tropical Atlantic) may limit generalizability; reliance on reanalysis data introduces inherent uncertainties; causal mechanisms underlying TPT pathways not mechanistically explained.

**TL;DR:**
Transition path theory reveals that deep thermoclines and fast translational speeds are stronger predictors of hurricane rapid intensification than barrier layer thickness alone.
