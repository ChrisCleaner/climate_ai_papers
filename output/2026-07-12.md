### [Evaluating the Generalizability of Foundation Models for Extreme Environmental Events: Case Study of California Wildfire PM2.5](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.07951v1)

**Authors:** Yongcan Huang, Li Jiang, Ze Yu Liu
**Published:** 2026-07-08
**Categories:** cs.LG, physics.ao-ph
**Relevance Score:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐

# Research Summary: Foundation Models for Wildfire PM2.5 Forecasting

**Main Contribution:**  
First systematic evaluation of time series foundation models (TSFMs) on extreme wildfire smoke events, revealing that smaller, fully-trained recurrent models outperform larger pretrained foundation models for hazardous PM2.5 spike prediction.

**Method:**  
Benchmarked six TSFM configurations (TimesFM, Chronos-2, Moirai-2, Time-MoE—both zero-shot and LoRA fine-tuned) against LSTM/BiLSTM/Transformer baselines using leave-one-incident-out cross-validation. Evaluated performance via MAE, RMSE, and exceedance F1 scores at EPA AQI thresholds across 6–24-hour forecast horizons.

**Data:**  
12-year hourly PM2.5 dataset (2013–2025) spanning 1,375 wildfire incidents across 79 California air quality monitoring sites.

**Climate Relevance:**  
Addresses critical gap in forecasting rare, extreme air quality events driven by climate-influenced wildfire activity; directly supports public health adaptation to smoke hazards.

**Implications:**  
Challenges the assumption that scale and pretraining universally improve environmental forecasting; recommends BiLSTM deployment for operational wildfire air quality prediction systems where exceedance detection is paramount.

**Limitations:**  
Single geographic region (California); limited to PM2.5 (not multivariate air quality); foundation model performance may improve with domain-specific pretraining or larger fine-tuning datasets.

**TL;DR:**  
Specialized recurrent models outperform general-purpose foundation models for forecasting extreme wildfire PM2.5 events, suggesting task-specific architectures remain essential for environmental extremes.

**Relevance Score:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

# Research Summary: Domain-Adaptive Climate Downscaling Under Temporal Distribution Shift

**Main Contribution:**
Addresses the temporal out-of-distribution (OOD) problem in climate downscaling by proposing a domain-adaptive deep learning framework that maintains model performance when applied to future climate projections with different statistical distributions than historical training data.

**Method:**
Combines supervised high-resolution (HR) reconstruction on historical data with domain alignment techniques to bridge the distributional gap between historical and future climate states. The framework explicitly accounts for non-stationary climate conditions during model transfer.

**Data:**
Paired low-resolution (LR) and high-resolution (HR) climate model simulations over the Continental United States, with daily temperature as the primary variable. Training on historical periods and validation on future projection periods.

**Climate Relevance:**
Critical for generating reliable high-resolution climate projections needed for regional impact assessments, adaptation planning, and extreme weather analysis. Temporal distribution shift is inherent to climate change scenarios where future conditions diverge from historical baselines.

**Implications:**
Improves operational downscaling workflows for climate services and impact modeling. Particularly valuable for mountainous and topographically complex regions where fine-scale projections are essential. Enhanced extreme temperature bias correction supports climate risk assessment.

**Limitations:**
Study focuses on daily temperature over one region; generalization to other variables (precipitation, wind) and geographic domains requires further validation. Computational cost of domain adaptation not discussed.

**TL;DR:**
Domain adaptation mitigates temporal distribution shift in deep learning-based climate downscaling, improving projection robustness under non-stationary climate conditions.

# Research Summary: Microseismicity and Lithology via Slip Avalanche Models

**Main Contribution:**
Demonstrates that a universal micromechanical model of slip avalanches predicts microseismic event statistics across vastly different scales (nanometers to meters) and materials, enabling prediction of earthquake sizes and timing during fluid injection operations.

**Method:**
• Physics-based slip avalanche model applied to slowly deforming solids
• Statistical analysis of seismic catalogs from high-pressure fluid injection
• Nanoindentation experiments on shale samples across temperature and mineral composition ranges
• Finite-size and stress-integrative effects used to explain deviations in layered formations

**Data:**
Microseismic catalogs from unconventional oil/gas and geothermal operations; nanoindentation experiments on shale samples with varying thermal and compositional conditions.

**Climate Relevance:**
Directly applicable to geologic carbon storage (CCS)—a critical climate mitigation technology—by improving safety and predictability of induced seismicity during CO₂ injection into subsurface formations.

**Implications:**
Enables risk assessment and operational optimization for geothermal energy, CCS projects, and unconventional energy extraction by predicting microseismic event probability distributions from basin structure and lithology.

**Limitations:**
Model validation limited to specific geological contexts; transferability to other injection scenarios or complex fault geometries not explicitly addressed.

**TL;DR:**
A universal slip avalanche model predicts induced microseismic statistics across scales and materials, improving safety predictions for subsurface fluid injection in climate-relevant applications like carbon storage.

**Implications:**
