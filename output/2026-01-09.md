# Research Summary: Carbon Mineralization in Basaltic Systems

**Main Contribution:**
Demonstrates that carbonate precipitation in basaltic rocks is nucleation-controlled and stochastic, with pore network topology—not just chemistry—being the primary control on mineralization efficiency and permeability evolution during CO₂ storage.

**Method:**
• Flow-through experiments (80°C) with CO₂-acidified seawater on basaltic glass
• Geochemical modeling (reactive transport simulation)
• Multi-scale pore imaging: micro-CT analysis and connected porosity quantification across three basalt facies

**Data:**
Connected porosity measurements (1.3–32% vs. total porosity 18–42%), pore coordination numbers (modal = 2), precipitation phase identification (calcium carbonate, smectite), permeability changes under variable flow rates (0.05–0.005 mL/min).

**Climate Relevance:**
Basaltic carbon mineralization offers permanent CO₂ sequestration as a climate mitigation strategy, with potential for rapid, geologically stable storage compared to conventional saline aquifer injection.

**Implications:**
Pore topology vulnerability in basalts creates risk of catastrophic permeability loss from modest precipitation; seawater chemistry reduces mineralization efficiency; probabilistic reactive transport models and realistic pore geometries are essential for designing effective basalt-hosted CCS operations.

**Limitations:**
Laboratory-scale experiments; limited to specific basalt types; seawater complexity may not capture all field conditions; long-term durability of precipitates not addressed.

**TL;DR:**
Basaltic CO₂ storage efficiency depends critically on pore network topology and nucleation stochasticity, not equilibrium chemistry alone, requiring probabilistic modeling frameworks for field deployment.

# Research Summary: Physics Suites in Fixed Dynamics Models

**Main Contribution:**
Isolates uncertainty sources in atmospheric models by decoupling sub-grid physics from large-scale dynamics. Demonstrates that different parameterization schemes produce surprisingly similar precipitation despite their structural differences, suggesting potential overconfidence in coarse-grid models.

**Method:**
• Fixed large-scale dynamics derived from convection-permitting ICON DYAMOND benchmark (2.5 km resolution)
• Four different physics suites forecast sub-grid tendencies at 22 km grid
• Joint probability distribution analysis of precipitation and physics tendencies
• One-month Indian Ocean case study with correlation and vertical tendency diagnostics

**Data:**
ICON DYAMOND convection-permitting experiment (2.5 km); MUMIP (Model Uncertainty Model Intercomparison Project) dataset with four parameterized physics suites at 22 km resolution.

**Climate Relevance:**
Precipitation is a primary source of model uncertainty and directly controls atmospheric heat redistribution—critical for climate projections. Understanding parameterization differences is essential for reducing climate model spread.

**Implications:**
High inter-model agreement at coarse resolution masks fundamental differences in unresolved convective processes. Results suggest current parameterizations may underestimate structural uncertainty, with implications for ensemble forecasting and climate model weighting strategies.

**Limitations:**
Single regional case study (Indian Ocean); limited to one month; doesn't fully explain mechanisms driving benchmark divergence; requires extension to global/seasonal scales.

**TL;DR:**
Coarse-grid physics suites show artificial agreement that masks real differences in convective parameterization, indicating potential overconfidence in operational and climate models.
