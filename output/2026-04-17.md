# Research Summary: Sea-Level Change from U.S. Vehicle Emissions

Extends EPA's vehicle emissions impact assessment using coupled climate-sea-level modeling with probabilistic uncertainty quantification. Demonstrates that transportation sector emissions reductions produce substantial long-term sea-level benefits (>6 cm by 2200) and regionally amplified coastal effects.

**Method:**
- FaIR climate model (emissions-to-temperature) coupled with BRICK sea-level model (temperature-to-sea-level)
- Probabilistic weighting approach for joint climate-sea-level uncertainty
- Extended timescale analysis (to 2200) beyond EPA's 2100 horizon
- Downscaling to U.S. coastal regions

**Data:**
EPA vehicle emissions scenarios; SSP2-4.5 baseline scenario; alternative scenarios including stalled technological progress and pre-regulation fleet counterfactual.

**Climate Relevance:**
Quantifies previously underestimated long-term sea-level rise contribution from transportation sector—a major U.S. emissions source. Reveals multi-century climate commitment and regional vulnerability patterns critical for coastal adaptation planning.

**Implications:**
Supports policy case for vehicle emissions regulations by demonstrating substantial cumulative benefits over multi-generational timescales. Gulf of Mexico coastal communities face disproportionate impacts, informing regional climate adaptation priorities.

**Limitations:**
Analysis focuses on U.S. emissions only; global transportation sector effects not addressed. Model uncertainty ranges not explicitly detailed; sensitivity to BRICK parameterization unclear.

**TL;DR:**
Vehicle emissions reductions yield modest near-term sea-level benefits (~1-2 cm by 2100) but substantial long-term gains (>6 cm by 2200) with amplified regional coastal impacts.

**Data:**
Global precipitation observations (93% of locations analyzed); CMIP6 multi-model ensemble projections under different Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs).

**Climate Relevance:**
Addresses critical gap in extreme precipitation forecasting by enabling probabilistic estimates of intensities beyond instrumental records—essential for infrastructure planning and risk assessment under climate change.

**Implications:**

- Provides actionable return period estimates for engineering design standards and climate adaptation planning
- Reveals scenario-independent scaling relationships that may improve cross-model precipitation projections
