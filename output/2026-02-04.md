# Research Summary: OPTiMEM and Heat Conjecture for Social Cost of Greenhouse Gases

**Main Contribution:**
Introduces OPTiMEM (Ocean-Heat-Content Physics and Time Macro Economic Model) coupled with a "heat conjecture" to estimate the social cost of carbon (SCC) as a dynamic economic phase space rather than single values. Proposes long-term carbon bonds as a mechanism to resolve discount rate disagreements in climate economics.

**Method:**
- Physics-based approach linking fossil fuel combustion → CO₂ emissions → ocean heat content (OHC) → weather damages
- 18 emission scenarios to quantify uncertainty ranges
- Stochastic damage functions proportional to OHC increases
- Risk models at 1:10, 1:100, and 1:1000 year loss frequencies
- Comparison with DICE model assumptions

**Data:**
GHG concentration records, temperature observations, ocean heat content measurements, fossil fuel consumption data.

**Climate Relevance:**
Directly addresses climate damages through ocean heat accumulation—the primary driver of long-term warming impacts. Extends SCC analysis beyond CO₂ to CH₄, N₂O, and halogenated hydrocarbons, capturing multi-gas climate forcing.

**Implications:**
- Provides risk-stratified metrics for policy and engineering decisions
- Suggests DICE models significantly underestimate long-term warming (18°C by 2210 CE vs. DICE projections)
- Highlights energy return constraints if low-cost fossil fuels deplete before renewable transition

**Limitations:**
- "Heat conjecture" (damage proportionality to OHC) lacks independent empirical validation
- Extreme temperature projections (+110°C by 2300 CE) warrant scrutiny regarding model assumptions
- Bond implementation mechanism underdeveloped; practical adoption unclear

**TL;DR:**
Physics-grounded model linking ocean heat to economic damages, proposing SCC as dynamic phase space and carbon bonds as discount-rate solution, with projections substantially exceeding conventional climate-economic models.

# Research Summary: Rapid SST Estimation from Sparse Observations

**Main Contribution:**
Introduces Sparse Discrete Empirical Interpolation Method (S-DEIM), a hybrid AI approach that reconstructs high-resolution global sea surface temperatures (SST) from sparse in situ observations without requiring physics-based models. Achieves 40% improvement over prior empirical methods while maintaining computational efficiency.

**Method:**
S-DEIM combines two components: (1) empirical interpolation from instantaneous sparse observations, and (2) a recurrent neural network (RNN) trained on historical SST patterns to capture temporal dynamics. The RNN learns spatiotemporal correlations from 33 years of weekly SST data, enabling reconstruction from only 0.2% of grid points.

**Data:**
Training: NOAA weekly high-resolution SST dataset (1989–2021). Testing: January 2022–January 2023 with 100 sparse in situ observations globally.

**Climate Relevance:**
SST is a critical variable for weather forecasting, seasonal climate predictions, and understanding ocean-atmosphere interactions. Sparse observational networks (buoys, ships) limit real-time monitoring; this method bridges the gap between sparse measurements and high-resolution fields needed for operational forecasting.

**Implications:**
Enables rapid, real-time SST field reconstruction for operational weather/climate centers. Computationally efficient (1-minute offline training; <1-second inference) makes it practical for streaming observational data. Robust to random sensor placement (1–2% error increase).

**Limitations:**
Test period limited to one year; robustness to extreme events (e.g., marine heatwaves) unclear. Generalization to other variables or regions not demonstrated. Sensor placement robustness tested only on random distributions.

**TL;DR:**
Hybrid RNN-empirical interpolation method reconstructs global SST fields from 0.2% sparse observations with 40% better accuracy than prior methods, enabling real-time operational deployment.
