# Research Summary: Carbon Mineralization in Basaltic CO₂ Storage

**Main Contribution:**
Demonstrates that carbonate precipitation in basaltic rocks is nucleation-controlled and stochastic, with pore network topology—not just chemistry—critically determining mineralization efficiency and permeability evolution during CO₂ storage.

**Method:**
• Flow-through experiments (80°C) with CO₂-acidified seawater on basaltic glass
• Geochemical modeling coupled with multi-scale pore imaging (micro-CT)
• Comparison of connected vs. total porosity across three basalt facies
• Analysis of pore coordination numbers and flow path vulnerability

**Data:**
Experimental precipitation phases (calcium carbonate, smectite); pore topology metrics from three basalt samples; flow rates (0.05–0.005 mL/min); porosity ranges (18–42% total, 1.3–32% connected).

**Climate Relevance:**
Basaltic carbon mineralization offers permanent, rapid CO₂ sequestration—a key negative-emissions pathway for climate mitigation—but operational feasibility depends on understanding permeability loss mechanisms.

**Implications:**
Pore architecture controls are as critical as geochemistry for CCS design; serial flow paths in basalts (coordination number ~2) risk catastrophic clogging from distributed small precipitates; seawater use reduces efficiency, requiring site-specific optimization.

**Limitations:**
Single-temperature experiments (80°C); limited to basaltic glass; seawater complexity may not generalize to all geological settings; probabilistic modeling frameworks remain underdeveloped.

**TL;DR:**
Basalt pore topology, not just mineral chemistry, governs CO₂ mineralization efficiency and permeability stability, necessitating probabilistic reactive transport models for viable subsurface storage.
