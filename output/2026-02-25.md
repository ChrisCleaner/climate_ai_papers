# AI-Based Regional Emulation for Kilometer-Scale Dynamical Downscaling

**Main Contribution:**
Develops an AI-based Limited-Area Model (LAM) that performs dynamical downscaling from 0.25° to 4-km resolution with strong generalization across diverse forcing datasets, time periods, and climate regimes. Demonstrates that weather prediction AI models can be effectively adapted for long-term regional climate applications.

**Method:**
Neural network trained to emulate regional atmospheric dynamics using coarse-resolution boundary conditions as input. Includes post-processing module for diagnostic variable derivation. Tested on present-day weather prediction, historical climate simulations, and future climate projections.

**Data:**
Training: ERA5 (0.25°, 3-hourly) as forcing; CONUS404 (4-km, hourly) as target (1980–2019). Evaluation: Multiple forcing datasets, non-ERA5 sources (1.0°, 6-hourly), global climate model outputs (1980–2010, 2070–2100), and ensemble runs (2025–2027).

**Climate Relevance:**
Enables high-resolution regional climate projections necessary for impact assessments in agriculture, water resources, and extreme weather. Addresses computational bottleneck of traditional dynamical downscaling for long climate simulations.

**Implications:**
Provides practical, transferable framework for rapid, cost-effective regional climate downscaling. Applicable to climate change impact studies and ensemble-based uncertainty quantification without ensemble spread collapse.

**Limitations:**
Evaluation limited to two U.S. regions (Southern Great Plains, southeastern U.S.); transferability to other regions/climates unclear. Long-term stability beyond 30-year simulations not demonstrated.

**TL;DR:**
AI-based regional weather model generalizes robustly across forcing datasets and climate regimes, enabling practical kilometer-scale climate downscaling for 30-year projections.

### [Weak 21st-century AMOC response to Greenland meltwater in a strongly eddying ocean model](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17235v1)

# Research Summary: AMOC Response to Greenland Meltwater

**Main Contribution:**
Quantifies Greenland meltwater's impact on Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) weakening through 2100 using a high-resolution eddy-resolving ocean model. Demonstrates that meltwater-induced AMOC weakening (0.6 ± 0.2 Sv) is modest compared to warming-driven weakening and is independent of model resolution.

**Method:**
High-resolution (1/10° horizontal resolution) ocean model simulation under SSP5-8.5 forcing scenario with explicit mesoscale eddy representation. Comparison between low and high-resolution configurations to isolate resolution-dependence effects and link AMOC changes to large-scale dynamical controls.

**Data:**
Climate model output from strongly eddying ocean simulations; SSP5-8.5 (high-emissions) forcing scenario; Greenland meltwater freshwater flux inputs through 2100.

**Climate Relevance:**
AMOC weakening is a critical tipping point in climate projections with major implications for Northern Hemisphere heat distribution and sea-level rise. Greenland ice sheet melt is an accelerating freshwater source; understanding its AMOC impact reduces structural uncertainty in climate projections.

**Implications:**
Suggests current climate models may overestimate meltwater's direct AMOC impact by neglecting eddies and background state-dependence. Indicates that ocean model resolution and climate state are critical for accurate AMOC projections in future scenarios.

**Limitations:**
Single model study; results may not generalize across different ocean model configurations; meltwater parameterization specifics not detailed.

**TL;DR:**
Greenland meltwater causes modest AMOC weakening in 21st-century projections, with effects determined more by background ocean state than model resolution.

# Research Summary: Generative Deep Learning for Historical Climate Reconstruction

**Main Contribution:**
Develops a probabilistic generative deep learning framework to reconstruct global historical temperature and precipitation fields from 1850 onward, overcoming smoothing artifacts and systematic biases in conventional statistical interpolation methods.

**Method:**
Uses a learned generative prior of Earth system dynamics to perform probabilistic inference on sparse observations, preserving higher-order statistics and generating uncertainty-aware spatiotemporally consistent climate fields rather than deterministic interpolations.

**Data:**
Historical instrumental observations from the early 20th century (sparse and fragmented); validated against widely-used reference products including those underlying IPCC assessments.

**Climate Relevance:**
Addresses critical gaps in instrumental climate records essential for accurately quantifying anthropogenic climate change, particularly for understanding historical extremes and variability in under-monitored regions (oceans, polar areas).

**Implications:**
Reveals previously undetected early 20th-century Arctic warming (0.15–0.29°C/decade higher than benchmarks for 1900–1980), suggests modern Arctic warming may be overestimated in some regions, identifies intense localized hotspots (Barents Sea, Northeastern Greenland), and documents intensification of the global hydrological cycle—findings with direct relevance to IPCC assessments and climate attribution studies.

**Limitations:**
Reliance on learned priors may introduce model-dependent biases; validation primarily against existing products rather than independent ground truth; computational requirements for global probabilistic inference not discussed.

**TL;DR:**
Generative AI reconstructs historically sparse climate data with better fidelity to extremes and variability, revealing previously hidden 20th-century warming patterns and hydrological intensification.
