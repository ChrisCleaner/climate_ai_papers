### [Scaling of Rainfall Intensity and Frequency with Rising Temperatures](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06408v1)

**Authors:** Jun Yin, Bei Gao, Amilcare Porporato
**Published:** 2026-01-10
**Categories:** physics.ao-ph
**Relevance Score:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐

# Research Summary: Rainfall Scaling with Temperature

**Main Contribution:**
Identifies a unifying physical mechanism explaining why extreme rainfall intensifies more than mean rainfall under warming. Demonstrates self-similar rainfall distributions across regions that link mean and extreme precipitation through temperature-dependent scaling laws.

**Method:**
- Integrates theoretical framework with empirical analysis from ~50,000 global rain-gauge stations
- Applies Clausius-Clapeyron (CC) scaling to characterize rainfall intensity response to temperature
- Analyzes rainfall frequency-intensity trade-offs constrained by atmospheric moisture dynamics and hydrological losses (runoff, percolation, evaporation)

**Data:**
Global network of nearly 50,000 rain-gauge stations spanning diverse geographical regions and multiple time periods.

**Climate Relevance:**
Directly addresses intensification of the hydrological cycle under global warming—a critical climate projection uncertainty. Explains the systematic divergence between mean and extreme precipitation responses, essential for understanding climate-driven flood and drought risks.

**Implications:**
- Provides robust scaling laws for translating temperature changes into precipitation shifts at local and global scales
- Enables improved climate projections and evidence-based adaptation planning for water resources and disaster risk management
- Quantifies the frequency-intensity trade-off governing future rainfall regimes

**Limitations:**
Not explicitly stated; potential constraints include rain-gauge measurement biases, spatial heterogeneity in updraft dynamics, and applicability of CC scaling under non-equilibrium conditions.

**TL;DR:**
Self-similar rainfall distributions and atmospheric moisture constraints create predictable scaling laws linking temperature to both rainfall intensity and frequency, explaining why extremes intensify faster than means.

### [Making Tunable Parameters State-Dependent in Weather and Climate Models with Reinforcement Learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04268v1)

**Authors:** Pritthijit Nath, Sebastian Schemm, Henry Moss...
**Published:** 2026-01-07
**Categories:** cs.LG, physics.ao-ph

# Research Summary: RL-Driven Tunable Parameters in Climate Models

**Main Contribution:**
Develops a reinforcement learning framework that learns parametrisation coefficients online as functions of model state, replacing traditional fixed offline-tuned parameters. Demonstrates this approach across multiple climate model hierarchies with superior performance to static tuning.

**Method:**
- Employs nine RL algorithms (TQC, DDPG, TD3 most effective) to optimize sub-grid parametrisation schemes
- Tests single-agent and federated multi-agent configurations across three model complexities: simple climate bias correction, radiative-convective equilibrium, and zonal mean energy balance model
- Evaluates using area-weighted RMSE, temperature profiles, and pressure-level diagnostics

**Data:**
Model-generated outputs from idealised testbeds; no external observational datasets explicitly mentioned.

**Climate Relevance:**
Addresses a fundamental limitation in weather and climate models: fixed parametrisation coefficients that poorly represent unresolved sub-grid physics (convection, radiation, etc.) and contribute to persistent model biases that degrade forecast skill and climate projections.

**Implications:**
Enables adaptive, state-dependent parametrisations that could improve model accuracy across different climate regimes; federated multi-agent approach offers scalable pathway for geographically specialised parameter control in operational models.

**Limitations:**
Evaluation restricted to idealised model hierarchies; scalability to full-complexity GCMs and computational overhead during online learning not addressed; generalization to unseen climate states unclear.

**TL;DR:**
Reinforcement learning enables climate models to learn physically-meaningful, state-dependent parametrisation coefficients online, outperforming traditional static tuning across multiple model complexities.

### [How different are deterministic physics suites when coupled to fixed model dynamics and why?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03393v1)

**Published:** 2026-01-06
**Categories:** physics.ao-ph

# Research Summary: Physics Suites in Fixed-Dynamics Atmospheric Models

**Main Contribution:**
Isolates the contribution of sub-grid physics parameterizations to model uncertainty by prescribing identical large-scale dynamics across four different physics suites. Reveals that coarse-grid models (22km) exhibit overconfidence in their physics tendencies compared to a convection-permitting benchmark (2.5km).

**Method:**
• Fixed dynamics framework: Large-scale flow prescribed from ICON DYAMOND convection-permitting simulation
• Four physics suites forecast sub-grid tendencies independently
• Joint probability density function (PDF) analysis of precipitation and physics tendencies
• Correlation analysis between models and benchmark at native/coarse grids

**Data:**
Indian Ocean experiment over one month; ICON DYAMOND benchmark at 2.5km resolution; four coarse-grid physics suites at 22km resolution from MUMIP dataset.

**Climate Relevance:**
Precipitation is a primary source of model uncertainty and controls atmospheric heat redistribution. Understanding physics parameterization differences is critical for improving weather and climate forecasts, particularly in tropical regions with active convection.

**Implications:**
Physics suites show artificially high agreement (r > 0.95) at coarse resolution, masking structural differences. The gap with the benchmark (r ≈ 0.80) suggests parameterized convection inadequately represents fine-scale dynamics, with implications for model calibration and ensemble design.

**Limitations:**
Single-month, single-region experiment; analysis limited to one variable (precipitation); mechanism for convective structure variation remains hypothetical.

**TL;DR:**
Coarse-grid physics parameterizations appear more similar to each other than to high-resolution reality, indicating systematic overconfidence in sub-grid process representation.

# Research Summary: Carbon Tax Distributional Impacts

**Main Contribution:**
Systematically reviews and meta-analyzes methodological choices in microsimulation models that estimate how carbon taxes affect different population groups. Identifies key modeling decisions that significantly influence whether carbon taxes appear regressive or progressive.

**Method:**
Meta-analysis using probit regression on 217 distributional impact estimates from 71 countries to quantify how specific modeling choices (tax design, emission coverage, data sources, behavioral assumptions, general equilibrium effects) affect regressivity outcomes.

**Data:**
217 distributional impact estimates extracted from peer-reviewed literature across 71 countries; underlying microsimulation models use household-level survey data (country-specific), with varying temporal coverage.

**Climate Relevance:**
Carbon taxation is a primary policy instrument for emissions reduction; understanding distributional impacts is critical for designing politically feasible and equitable climate mitigation strategies that don't disproportionately burden low-income households.

**Implications:**
- Carbon border adjustment mechanisms may reduce regressivity
- Behavioral responses and general equilibrium effects substantially alter distributional outcomes
- Model standardization needed for policy comparisons
- Policymakers should account for methodological sensitivity when interpreting carbon tax equity analyses

**Limitations:**
Meta-analysis limited to published estimates (potential publication bias); heterogeneous modeling approaches complicate direct comparisons; results reflect available literature rather than optimal methodological practices.

**TL;DR:**
Modeling choices in carbon tax microsimulations significantly influence distributional impact estimates, with emission coverage scope and behavioral assumptions being critical determinants of regressivity findings.
