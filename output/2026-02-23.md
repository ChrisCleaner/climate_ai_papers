### [Weak 21st-century AMOC response to Greenland meltwater in a strongly eddying ocean model](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17235v1)

**Authors:** Oliver Mehling, Henk A. Dijkstra
**Published:** 2026-02-19
**Categories:** physics.ao-ph
**Relevance Score:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐

# Research Summary: AMOC Response to Greenland Meltwater

**Main Contribution:**
Quantifies the impact of Greenland Ice Sheet meltwater on Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) weakening through 2100 using a high-resolution ocean model. Demonstrates that meltwater-induced AMOC weakening (0.6 ± 0.2 Sv) is modest compared to warming-driven weakening and is largely independent of model resolution.

**Method:**
High-resolution (1/10° horizontal resolution) strongly eddying ocean model forced with SSP5-8.5 emissions scenario through 2100. Compares AMOC response at both high and low resolution to isolate effects of mesoscale eddy representation versus large-scale ocean dynamics.

**Data:**
SSP5-8.5 climate forcing scenario; Greenland meltwater input parameterized within the model framework; model output analyzed through 2100.

**Climate Relevance:**
AMOC weakening is a critical tipping point in the climate system affecting heat distribution, sea level, and regional climate across the Atlantic basin. Greenland ice sheet melt is an accelerating freshwater source under anthropogenic warming, making its AMOC impact essential for 21st-century projections.

**Implications:**
Results suggest that most climate models' omission of Greenland meltwater may not substantially underestimate AMOC decline, though the background ocean state significantly modulates meltwater sensitivity. Indicates that improving ocean eddy representation may be less critical than accurately capturing large-scale circulation controls for AMOC projections.

**Limitations:**
Single model study; meltwater parameterization methodology not detailed; results specific to SSP5-8.5 scenario; state-dependence findings may not generalize across different climate models.

**TL;DR:**
Greenland meltwater causes modest additional AMOC weakening in a high-resolution model, with effects determined more by ocean state than model resolution.

# Research Summary: Fast Radiative Feedbacks via ML Weather Emulators

Demonstrates that machine-learning weather emulators trained on historical data can accurately simulate fast radiative feedbacks (atmospheric processes operating on weekly timescales) without retraining, enabling study of climate responses to CO₂ perturbations outside the historical record.

**Method:**
• ML-based weather emulators trained on historical meteorological reanalyses
• Applied to radiative-convective equilibrium (RCE) simulations with perturbed CO₂ concentrations
• Validation against full-physics Earth System Models (ESMs)
• No retraining on future/non-historical boundary conditions

**Data:**
Historical meteorological reanalyses (specific datasets not detailed in abstract); comparison benchmarks from Earth System Models.

**Climate Relevance:**
Fast feedbacks (clouds, water vapor, atmospheric circulation) operate on timescales already present in observational records and directly influence the global hydrological cycle and precipitation response to greenhouse gas forcing—critical for near-term climate projections.

**Implications:**

Approach limited to fast feedbacks; slow feedbacks (ice-albedo, vegetation changes) require different treatment; generalization to non-equilibrium transient scenarios unclear from abstract.

**TL;DR:**
Historically trained ML weather emulators can quantify fast radiative feedback responses to CO₂ changes without retraining, validated against ESMs.

### [Ensemble-size-dependence of deep-learning post-processing methods that minimize an (un)fair score: motivating examples and a proof-of-concept solution](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.15830v1)

**Authors:** Christopher David Roberts
**Published:** 2026-02-17
**Categories:** physics.ao-ph, cs.LG
**Relevance Score:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐

# Research Summary: Ensemble-Size-Dependent Deep Learning for Fair Climate Forecasting

**Main Contribution:**
Identifies a critical fairness problem in deep-learning post-processing of ensemble forecasts: standard methods (linear calibration, transformer self-attention) become unreliable when ensemble size changes. Proposes trajectory transformers as a solution that maintains forecast reliability across different ensemble sizes.

**Method:**
- Compares two approaches that minimize adjusted continuous ranked probability score (aCRPS): member-by-member linear calibration and transformer self-attention across ensemble dimension
- Introduces trajectory transformers that apply self-attention over lead time instead of ensemble members, preserving conditional independence assumptions required by fair scoring rules
- Tests on ECMWF subseasonal forecasts of 2-meter temperature (T₂m)

**Data:**
ECMWF subseasonal forecasting system weekly mean T₂m predictions; experiments with training ensembles of 3 vs. 9 members and real-time forecasts of 9 vs. 100 members.

**Climate Relevance:**
Addresses practical challenges in subseasonal climate prediction where computational constraints limit ensemble size. Improves reliability of bias-corrected forecasts critical for seasonal climate applications.

**Implications:**
Enables flexible deployment of post-processed ensemble forecasts without retraining when operational ensemble sizes change; maintains forecast skill and reliability across different configurations.

**Limitations:**
Evaluation limited to single variable (T₂m) and one forecast system; generalization to other variables, regions, or lead times not demonstrated.

**TL;DR:**
Deep-learning ensemble post-processing methods can become unreliable when ensemble size changes; trajectory transformers solve this by applying self-attention over time rather than ensemble members.

**Published:** 2026-02-17

# Research Summary: Southern Ocean Latent Heat Flux Variability

**Main Contribution:**
Quantifies how ocean mesoscale (100 km) and submesoscale (1-10 km) features drive latent heat flux variability in the Southern Ocean, showing these fine-scale processes account for up to 80% of LHF variance in eddy-rich regions—a mechanism largely unresolved in current climate models and reanalysis products.

**Method:**
Year-long, fully coupled ocean-atmosphere simulation at kilometer-scale resolution with spectral analysis to decompose LHF variance across spatial scales; comparison with ERA5 reanalysis to quantify observational gaps.

**Data:**
Global coupled simulation output (1 km resolution); ERA5 reanalysis for validation; observational focus on Southern Ocean eddy-rich and eddy-poor regions.

**Climate Relevance:**
Latent heat flux is critical for ocean-atmosphere heat and moisture exchange. The Southern Ocean drives global climate through thermohaline circulation, making accurate representation of air-sea coupling essential for climate projections.

**Implications:**
Current climate models and reanalysis significantly underestimate fine-scale ocean forcing on the atmosphere (~17% of variance missing). Improved parameterization or higher-resolution modeling of meso-/submesoscale processes could improve Southern Ocean representation and potentially refine climate predictions.

**Limitations:**
Single-year simulation; focus on Southern Ocean limits generalizability; non-local atmospheric response mechanism requires further investigation.

**TL;DR:**
Ocean mesoscale and submesoscale features drive most Southern Ocean latent heat flux variability but remain unresolved in climate models, potentially biasing air-sea coupling representation.

# Research Summary: Generative Deep Learning for Historical Climate Reconstruction

**Main Contribution:**
Develops a probabilistic generative deep learning framework that reconstructs global historical climate fields (temperature and precipitation) from 1850 onward with improved fidelity over conventional statistical methods. The approach recovers spatiotemporal consistency and preserves climate variability statistics, revealing previously hidden historical extremes and regional patterns.

**Method:**
Uses a learned generative prior of Earth system dynamics to perform probabilistic inference on sparse observations, enabling uncertainty-aware reconstruction. The framework leverages generative models to enforce physical consistency rather than relying on disparate statistical interpolation techniques.

**Data:**
Historical instrumental observations from the early 20th century onward; validation against widely-used reference products (including those underlying IPCC assessments).

**Climate Relevance:**
Directly addresses a critical gap in climate science: sparse, fragmented early instrumental records limit confidence in anthropogenic warming attribution and baseline variability estimates. Improved reconstructions are essential for accurate climate change detection and attribution studies.

**Implications:**
- Reveals 0.15–0.29°C/decade higher Arctic warming (1900–1980) than existing products
- Identifies previously unresolved regional hotspots (Barents Sea, Northeastern Greenland)
- Suggests modern Arctic warming may be overestimated in recent assessments
- Demonstrates intensification of the global hydrological cycle with better ocean/remote region coverage

**Limitations:**
Apparent reliance on learned priors; validation primarily against existing products rather than independent observational networks; generalization to pre-1850 periods unclear.

**TL;DR:**
Generative AI reconstructs 1850–present global climate fields with higher fidelity, revealing stronger early 20th-century Arctic warming and previously undetected regional extremes.
