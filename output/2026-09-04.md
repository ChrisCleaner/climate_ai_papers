**Authors:** Paolo Giani, Arlene M. Fiore, Raffaele Ferrari...

**Categories:** physics.ao-ph

# Research Summary: Land-Ocean Warming Contrast in Climate Models

**Main Contribution:**
Demonstrates that the land-ocean warming contrast (differential surface warming between land and ocean) emerges from complementary radiative and dynamical controls operating through atmospheric moist static energy (MSE) transport. Develops an interpretable emulator that successfully reproduces this response across 22 CMIP6 models and reveals two distinct model regimes linked to climate sensitivity.

**Method:**
- Framework: Unified MSE transport perspective connecting energy balance (radiative) and atmospheric dynamics (dynamical) constraints
- Emulator construction: Interpretable model trained on CMIP6 ensemble to predict land-ocean warming response
- Analysis: Decomposition of radiative feedbacks and MSE-transport feedbacks (~0.2 PW/K) across model regimes

**Data:**
CMIP6 coupled climate models (n=22); surface air temperature, radiative fluxes, and atmospheric circulation fields from greenhouse forcing simulations.

**Climate Relevance:**
Land-ocean warming contrast is a fundamental feature of climate change with implications for regional precipitation patterns, extreme heat events, and ecosystem responses. Understanding its physical drivers improves confidence in regional climate projections.

**Implications:**
- Identifies climate sensitivity as a key predictor of inter-model spread in warming contrast
- Provides diagnostic framework for evaluating model fidelity in representing land-ocean differential warming
- Suggests targeted improvements to radiative feedback and dynamical transport parameterizations

**Limitations:**
Analysis limited to CMIP6 ensemble; emulator interpretability depends on framework assumptions; real-world validation against observations not explicitly discussed.

**TL;DR:**
Land-ocean warming contrast arises from competing radiative feedbacks and a robust MSE-transport restoring mechanism, with model predictions clustered into two regimes aligned with climate sensitivity.

# Research Summary: Surface Ocean Carbon Representation in Models and Observations

**Main Contribution:**
Introduces a manifold-based framework using intrinsic dimension and differentiable information imbalance to quantify how well ocean carbon observations and models represent the underlying data complexity. Reveals systematic gaps between SOCAT observations and global ocean biogeochemistry models (GOBMs) while validating the relative importance of learned variables.

**Method:**
• Manifold learning approach analyzing intrinsic dimensionality of data spaces
• Differentiable information imbalance metric to compare observation-model representation quality
• Machine learning optimization to derive optimal feature representations for ocean carbon
• Comparative analysis of variable rankings across datasets

**Data:**
SOCAT (Surface Ocean CO₂ Atlas) observational database and outputs from multiple global ocean biogeochemistry models (GOBMs).

**Climate Relevance:**
Surface ocean carbon is critical for understanding the ocean's role in the global carbon cycle and CO₂ uptake capacity—essential for climate projections and carbon budget assessments.

**Implications:**
Enables construction of improved weighted ensemble models for ocean carbon estimation; identifies regional deficiencies (e.g., Southern Ocean) requiring model refinement; provides quantitative metrics for systematic model-observation comparison and validation.

**Limitations:**
Analysis limited to most widely used feature sets; regional accuracy issues unresolved; no significant improvement in model representation over two decades suggests structural rather than incremental model limitations.

**TL;DR:**
A manifold-based framework reveals that ocean biogeochemistry models capture variable relationships correctly but incompletely represent observational data complexity, with persistent regional gaps.

# Research Summary: UODEs for Warm-Rain Parameterization

**Main Contribution:**
Applies Universal Ordinary Differential Equations (UODEs) to learn cloud microphysical closures for warm-rain formation directly from simulation data. The framework successfully reproduces kinetic collection equation (KCE) trajectories and reveals structural insights into existing parameterizations, enabling analytical improvements.

**Method:**
• Combines neural networks with ODEs to learn dynamical operators from time series
• Trains on super-droplet model trajectories (KCE approximation)
• Uses only prognostic state variables as inputs—no explicit autoconversion/accretion rate targets
• Analyzes learned operator to interpret and refine the Seifert-Beheng (2001) parameterization

**Data:**
Super-droplet model simulations approximating the kinetic collection equation for warm-rain processes.

**Climate Relevance:**
Warm-rain parameterization is fundamental to cloud microphysics in climate and weather models. Improved closures enhance precipitation prediction accuracy, critical for climate projections and extreme weather forecasting.

**Implications:**
UODEs serve dual roles: (1) accurate surrogate models for computationally expensive microphysical processes, and (2) interpretable tools for diagnosing and refining analytical parameterizations. The refined formulation improves KCE agreement while maintaining operational simplicity.

**Limitations:**
Generalization beyond training domain not explicitly tested; applicability to other cloud regimes (e.g., ice processes) unclear; computational cost of UODE inference relative to analytical formulas not discussed.

**TL;DR:**
Machine learning-augmented differential equations reveal that empirical accretion suppression in cloud parameterizations primarily corrects autoconversion overestimation, enabling more accurate analytical formulations.

# Research Summary: Cold Extremes during Dansgaard-Oeschger Oscillations

**Main Contribution:**
Quantifies how extreme cold temperatures change during abrupt climate oscillations by fitting statistical extreme value models to simulated Last Glacial Maximum conditions. Demonstrates that extreme value distribution parameters scale linearly with Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) strength within climate states, with nonlinear transitions between states.

**Method:**
Non-stationary Generalized Extreme Value (GEV) distributions fitted to surface temperature extremes, with comparison to nonlinear models. Parameters (location, scale, shape) mapped as functions of AMOC strength to characterize regional extremal behavior during stadial-interstadial transitions.

**Data:**
CCSM4 climate model simulation of Last Glacial Maximum conditions exhibiting Dansgaard-Oeschger-like rapid oscillations between cold (stadial) and warm (interstadial) states.

**Climate Relevance:**
Addresses how extreme cold events respond to abrupt climate shifts driven by ocean circulation changes—directly relevant to understanding paleoclimate variability and potential future rapid climate transitions. Sea ice expansion/retreat and current strength changes create regional teleconnections affecting extremes globally.

**Implications:**
Provides a quantitative framework for comparing extreme value statistics across climate models and states; enables paleoclimate proxy validation; offers methodology for assessing extreme event changes under different AMOC scenarios.

**Limitations:**
Results specific to Last Glacial Maximum conditions; model-dependent findings; comparison with proxies noted as requiring careful attention to dynamical state and location.

**TL;DR:**
Extreme cold temperatures scale predictably with AMOC strength during abrupt climate oscillations, enabling model intercomparison through GEV parameter mapping.

# Research Summary: AI Downscaling of Atlantic Hurricanes

**Main Contribution:**
Develops an AI-based dynamical downscaling system that converts coarse-resolution hurricane boundary conditions into kilometer-scale, hourly weather fields with probabilistic uncertainty quantification via generative ensembles.

**Method:**
• Limited-area AI model: autoregressively downscales 3-hourly low-resolution forcings to hourly high-resolution fields
• Diffusion model: generates ensemble members of hazard-relevant variables from deterministic outputs
• Training: supervised learning on regridded CONUS404 data with ERA5 forcings

**Data:**
CONUS404 (reference dataset), ERA5 (training forcings), GDAS/FNL (alternative forcing models), 20 Atlantic hurricanes (2020–2024 validation period).

**Climate Relevance:**
Addresses critical gap in hurricane prediction: resolving sub-grid-scale extremes (eyewall intensity, rainfall rates, storm surge precursors) essential for climate impact assessment and adaptation planning in coastal regions.

**Implications:**
Enables rapid, computationally efficient ensemble hurricane forecasting at convection-permitting resolution; applicable to climate projections and operational forecasting with reduced computational cost versus traditional dynamical models.

**Limitations:**
Evaluation limited to 20 recent cases; generalization to future climate conditions or different ocean basins not demonstrated; dependence on training data quality (CONUS404) not thoroughly analyzed.

**TL;DR:**
Machine learning downscales hurricane forecasts from regional to kilometer-scale resolution with probabilistic ensembles, capturing extreme weather structures while maintaining computational efficiency.
