# Research Summary: Global Radiative Feedback Weakening

**Main Contribution:**
The study demonstrates that Earth's climate stability (quantified by radiative feedback parameter λ) has weakened significantly since the mid-1990s, shifting from maximum stability (~-3 W m⁻² K⁻¹) to reduced stability (~-2 W m⁻² K⁻¹). This represents a concerning trend in the climate system's ability to self-regulate temperature changes.

**Method:**
A convolutional neural network (CNN) trained on climate model simulations was applied to observation-based surface temperature reconstructions to estimate radiative feedback variations through 2025. Results were validated against climate model simulations extended to 2022 and attribution analysis identified regional drivers.

**Data:**
- Climate model simulations (training dataset for CNN)
- Observation-based surface temperature reconstructions
- CMIP6 model outputs extended to 2022
- E3SMv2 targeted experiments for attribution

**Climate Relevance:**
The radiative feedback parameter is fundamental to climate sensitivity—a weaker feedback means the climate system responds more strongly to forcing, amplifying warming. Subtropical Northeast Pacific warming is identified as a key regional driver of this destabilization.

**Implications:**
Enables near real-time monitoring of climate stability using AI, providing early warning of changes in Earth's climate response. Suggests the climate system may be transitioning toward higher sensitivity to perturbations.

**Limitations:**
CNN training relies on model simulations with potential systematic biases; observation-based reconstructions have inherent uncertainties; attribution to specific regions requires confirmation through additional modeling experiments.

**TL;DR:**
AI-enhanced analysis reveals Earth's climate feedback has weakened ~33% since 1995, reducing the system's stability and amplifying warming responses.

# FloeNet: Sea Ice ML Emulator Summary

**Main Contribution:**
FloeNet is a mass-conserving neural network emulator that reproduces sea ice dynamics from the GFDL SIS2 model with high fidelity across different climate states. It demonstrates superior performance to non-conservative models while maintaining physical interpretability through explicit conservation laws.

**Method:**
• Neural network trained on 6-hour mass and area budget tendencies (growth, melt, advection)
• Mass conservation constraint embedded in architecture
• Outputs both ice/snow state variables and coupling fluxes (skin temperature, salt flux, melt energy)

**Data:**
Trained on reanalysis-forced ice-ocean simulations; tested on pre-industrial control and 1% CO₂ warming scenarios from GFDL SIS2.

**Climate Relevance:**
Sea ice is a critical climate feedback mechanism; accurate emulation of polar processes (thermodynamic vs. dynamic responses) is essential for understanding climate sensitivity and polar amplification.

**Implications:**
FloeNet can accelerate climate modeling by replacing computationally expensive ice models in coupled simulations; improved polar physics in Earth system emulators could enhance climate projections and uncertainty quantification.

**Limitations:**
Generalization tested only across two climate scenarios; computational speedup relative to SIS2 not explicitly quantified; applicability to extreme climate states (e.g., ice-free Arctic) unclear.

**TL;DR:**
A physics-informed neural network emulator of sea ice dynamics that conserves mass, generalizes across climates, and produces interpretable coupling variables for climate modeling.
