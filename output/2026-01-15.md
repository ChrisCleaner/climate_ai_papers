# Residual Power Flow for Neural Solvers – Summary

**Main Contribution:**
Introduces Residual Power Flow (RPF), a foundational neural solver formulation that learns to minimize residual functions based on Kirchhoff's laws to solve power flow equations. RPF improves both computational speed and flexibility compared to task-specific neural solvers, enabling reusable solutions across multiple grid operational tasks.

**Method:**
- Formulates power flow as residual minimization (infeasibility quantification via Kirchhoff's laws)
- Integrates neural solver within Predict-then-Optimise (PO) framework: neural network predicts voltage solutions, optimization refines for AC-feasibility
- Adds slack variable to achieve constraint satisfaction
- Trains on residual-based loss rather than direct power flow outputs

**Data:**
IEEE 9-bus test system; specific dataset sizes not detailed in abstract.

**Climate Relevance:**
Directly addresses energy transition operational challenges by enabling fast, flexible grid simulations under high renewable penetration. Renewable uncertainty requires rapid re-optimization; neural solvers accelerate this without sacrificing feasibility.

**Implications:**
Enables real-time grid operation support for renewable-heavy systems; reduces computational bottlenecks in dispatch, security assessment, and contingency analysis. Reusable foundation reduces need for task-specific model retraining.

**Limitations:**
Validation limited to small test system (9-bus); scalability to realistic transmission/distribution networks unclear. Generalization across different grid topologies not demonstrated.

**TL;DR:**
RPF enables fast, flexible neural approximations of power flow solvers by learning residual-based formulations, improving both speed and reusability for renewable-integrated grid operations.

# Research Summary: Learning Stochastic Differential Equations for Tropical Cyclone Intensification

**Main Contribution:**
Demonstrates that interpretable, physics-based differential equation models of tropical cyclone intensification can be automatically learned from data rather than hand-derived. The learned 10-term cubic stochastic differential equation captures realistic intensification dynamics while remaining computationally tractable for ensemble simulations.

**Method:**
• System identification to learn a stochastic differential equation (SDE) from observational data
• Model incorporates engineered environmental features (wind shear, sea surface temperature, etc.) known to drive intensification
• Trained on high-quality intensity observations paired with environmental reanalysis data
• Generates synthetic intensity time series for statistical validation

**Data:**
• IBTrACS hurricane intensity records (Northern Hemisphere)
• ERA5 reanalysis for large-scale environmental conditions
• Restricted to Northern Hemisphere storms

**Climate Relevance:**
Addresses critical gap in tropical cyclone hazard quantification by enabling physics-informed synthetic ensemble generation. Improves risk assessment for hurricane-prone regions where historical records are limited in size and quality.

**Implications:**
Enables rapid generation of large synthetic hurricane ensembles for probabilistic hazard estimation and climate risk modeling. Provides interpretable alternative to black-box ML models while maintaining predictive skill comparable to physics-based approaches.

**Limitations:**
Northern Hemisphere only; validation limited to statistical properties rather than individual storm prediction; dependence on quality of reanalysis environmental data not fully explored.

**TL;DR:**
Machine learning discovers a simple, interpretable differential equation model of hurricane intensification that generates realistic synthetic storms for hazard assessment.

# Research Summary: Rainfall Scaling with Temperature

**Main Contribution:**
Identifies a unified physical mechanism explaining why extreme rainfall intensifies faster than mean rainfall under global warming. Demonstrates self-similar rainfall distributions across regions that link mean and extreme precipitation through temperature-dependent scaling laws.

**Method:**
- Integrates theoretical framework with empirical analysis from ~50,000 rain-gauge stations globally
- Applies Clausius-Clapeyron (CC) scaling to characterize rainfall intensity response to temperature
- Analyzes rainfall frequency-intensity trade-offs constrained by atmospheric moisture dynamics and hydrological losses (runoff, percolation, evaporation)

**Data:**
Global rain-gauge network comprising nearly 50,000 stations across diverse geographical regions and multiple time periods.

**Climate Relevance:**
Directly addresses how the hydrological cycle intensifies with warming—a critical uncertainty in climate projections. Explains the disproportionate amplification of extreme precipitation relative to mean rainfall, which drives flood risk and ecosystem stress.

**Implications:**
- Provides robust scaling laws for translating temperature changes into precipitation shifts at local and global scales
- Enables improved climate projections for precipitation extremes
- Supports evidence-based adaptation planning for water resource management and disaster risk reduction

**Limitations:**
Not explicitly stated; potential constraints include rain-gauge spatial coverage gaps, temporal resolution limitations, and applicability of CC scaling under non-equilibrium conditions.

**TL;DR:**
Self-similar rainfall distributions and atmospheric moisture constraints explain why extreme precipitation intensifies faster than mean rainfall with warming, enabling predictive scaling laws for climate adaptation.

# Research Summary: Carbon Tax Distributional Impacts

**Main Contribution:**
Systematically reviews and meta-analyzes methodological choices in microsimulation models that estimate how carbon taxes affect different population groups. Identifies key modeling decisions that significantly influence whether carbon taxes appear regressive or progressive.

**Method:**
Meta-analysis using probit regression on 217 distributional impact estimates from 71 countries to quantify how modeling choices (tax design, emission coverage, data sources, behavioral assumptions, general equilibrium effects) influence regressivity findings.

**Data:**
217 estimates from 71 countries across published microsimulation studies of carbon tax distributional impacts; analysis covers diverse datasets and time periods used in original studies.

**Climate Relevance:**
Carbon taxation is a primary policy instrument for emissions reduction, but political viability depends on perceived fairness; understanding distributional impacts is critical for designing equitable climate policy that maintains public support.

**Implications:**
- Carbon border adjustment mechanisms may reduce regressivity concerns
- Behavioral responses and general equilibrium effects substantially alter distributional outcomes
- Standardized modeling frameworks needed for policy comparison
- Tax design choices (progressivity mechanisms) can mitigate regressive impacts

**Limitations:**
Meta-analysis cannot fully account for heterogeneity in underlying assumptions; causality between modeling choices and regressivity findings remains partially confounded; results depend on quality and comparability of included studies.

**TL;DR:**
Modeling choices in carbon tax impact assessments significantly influence regressivity findings, with carbon border mechanisms and behavioral accounting reducing regressive outcomes.
