### [Scaling of Rainfall Intensity and Frequency with Rising Temperatures](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06408v1)

**Categories:** physics.ao-ph
**Relevance Score:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐

# Research Summary: Rainfall Scaling with Temperature

**Main Contribution:**
Identifies a unifying physical mechanism explaining why extreme rainfall intensifies faster than mean rainfall under global warming. Demonstrates self-similar rainfall distributions across regions that link mean and extreme precipitation through temperature-dependent scaling laws.

**Method:**
- Integrates theoretical hydrological modeling with empirical data analysis
- Applies Clausius-Clapeyron (CC) scaling framework to characterize rainfall intensity response
- Analyzes rainfall frequency-intensity trade-offs driven by atmospheric moisture depletion dynamics

**Data:**
Global network of ~50,000 rain-gauge stations across diverse geographical regions and time periods.

**Climate Relevance:**
Explains the hydrological cycle intensification under warming and quantifies the disproportionate increase in extreme precipitation—a critical climate impact for flood risk and water security. The identified scaling laws bridge local observations to global climate projections.

**Implications:**
- Enables more accurate extreme precipitation forecasts in climate models
- Provides quantitative framework for adaptation planning in water resource management and infrastructure design
- Reveals fundamental trade-off: increased rainfall intensity comes with reduced rainfall frequency, affecting drought-flood patterns

**Limitations:**
Paper does not explicitly discuss regional variations in scaling behavior or potential non-stationarities under extreme warming scenarios beyond current observational ranges.

**TL;DR:**
Rainfall intensity and frequency follow predictable temperature-dependent scaling laws driven by atmospheric moisture dynamics, enabling improved climate projections of extreme precipitation.

### [Making Tunable Parameters State-Dependent in Weather and Climate Models with Reinforcement Learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04268v1)

**Authors:** Pritthijit Nath, Sebastian Schemm, Henry Moss...
**Published:** 2026-01-07
**Categories:** cs.LG, physics.ao-ph
**Relevance Score:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

# Research Summary: RL-Based State-Dependent Parametrization in Climate Models

**Main Contribution:**
Demonstrates a reinforcement learning framework that learns tunable parametrization coefficients online as functions of model state, replacing traditional fixed-coefficient schemes. Shows that RL-driven parameters outperform static tuning across idealized climate models and produce physically interpretable corrections.

**Method:**
Compares nine RL algorithms (TQC, DDPG, TD3 most effective) in single-agent and federated multi-agent configurations. Agents learn state-dependent parameter adjustments evaluated against static baselines using area-weighted RMSE and vertical temperature diagnostics across three model hierarchies (SCBC, RCE, EBM).

**Data:**
Evaluates performance on three idealized testbeds: Simple Climate Bias Correction (SCBC), Radiative-Convective Equilibrium (RCE), and Zonal Mean Energy Balance Model (EBM). No external datasets mentioned; experiments use synthetic model output.

**Climate Relevance:**
Addresses a fundamental limitation in weather/climate modeling: sub-grid parametrizations with weakly-constrained, offline-tuned coefficients that cannot adapt to evolving atmospheric conditions. State-dependent parameters could reduce persistent model biases that degrade forecast skill and climate projections.

**Implications:**
Offers a scalable pathway for online learning in operational numerical weather prediction and climate models. Multi-agent federated approach enables geographically specialized control, potentially improving regional forecast accuracy and reducing computational overhead.

**Limitations:**
Evaluation restricted to idealized models; scalability to full-complexity GCMs and computational cost of online RL training remain unaddressed. Generalization to unseen climate states and long-term stability beyond training periods unclear.

**TL;DR:**
Reinforcement learning enables climate models to learn adaptive, state-dependent parametrizations online, outperforming traditional fixed coefficients while producing physically meaningful corrections.

# Research Summary: Carbon Tax Distributional Impacts

**Main Contribution:**
Systematically reviews and meta-analyzes methodological choices in microsimulation models that assess how carbon taxes affect different population groups. Identifies key modeling decisions that significantly influence whether carbon taxes appear regressive or progressive.

**Method:**
- Systematic literature review of microsimulation modeling practices
- Probit regression meta-analysis on 217 distributional impact estimates across 71 countries
- Examines how modeling choices (tax design, emission coverage, data sources, behavioral responses) affect regressivity outcomes

**Data:**
217 estimates from 71 countries extracted from peer-reviewed literature; underlying studies use national household survey data and microsimulation platforms (varies by country).

**Climate Relevance:**
Carbon taxation is a primary policy instrument for emissions reduction, but political viability depends on perceived fairness. Understanding distributional impacts is critical for designing equitable climate policy that maintains public support.

**Implications:**
- Carbon border adjustment mechanisms may reduce regressivity by taxing imported emissions
- Accounting for household behavioral responses and income inequality measures yields less regressive estimates
- General equilibrium effects increase likelihood of finding regressive impacts
- No standardized modeling approach exists; results are sensitive to methodological choices

**Limitations:**
Meta-analysis cannot fully isolate causal effects of individual modeling choices due to interdependencies; publication bias toward significant findings possible; heterogeneity in country contexts limits generalizability.

**TL;DR:**
Distributional impact estimates of carbon taxes vary substantially across models; methodological choices—particularly emission coverage scope and behavioral assumptions—significantly influence whether taxes appear regressive.
