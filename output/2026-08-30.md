**Authors:** Victor Nascimento Ribeiro, Jorge Guevara, Jorge Sebastian Moraga...

**Categories:** cs.CV, cs.LG, physics.ao-ph

# Research Summary: Precipitation Downscaling Using Foundation Model-Conditioned Diffusion

**Main Contribution:**
Compares three conditioning strategies for diffusion models in statistical downscaling of precipitation, demonstrating that cross-attention conditioning—particularly with pretrained foundation models—outperforms simple concatenation for generating realistic high-resolution precipitation fields with improved extreme event representation.

**Method:**
Denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPMs) with three conditioning approaches: (1) channel concatenation of upsampled coarse predictors, (2) cross-attention with learned convolutional encoder, (3) cross-attention with frozen Prithvi WxC weather foundation model encoder. Evaluated using probabilistic (CRPS, MSE), distributional, spectral, and extreme-event metrics.

**Data:**
Daily precipitation observations and global climate model outputs for the Colorado River Basin; foundation model trained on 5 years vs. full dataset for learned encoder.

**Climate Relevance:**
Addresses critical gap between coarse global climate model outputs (~100 km resolution) and fine-scale hydrological impact assessments requiring high-resolution precipitation fields. Essential for water resource management and flood/drought risk assessment.

**Implications:**
Foundation model conditioning enables effective downscaling with limited training data, reducing computational burden while maintaining performance. Cross-attention preserves extreme precipitation events better than concatenation, improving reliability for hazard assessment.

**Limitations:**
Limited extreme event samples restrict uncertainty quantification for >100 mm/day events; evaluation confined to single basin; computational cost comparison incomplete for foundation model approach.

**TL;DR:**
Cross-attention diffusion models, especially with pretrained foundation models, effectively downscale precipitation while preserving extremes and requiring less training data than traditional approaches.

# AI and Climate Change: Integrated Assessment Summary

**Main Contribution:**
Develops a unified analytical framework integrating AI's emissions, economic output, and climate damages into the DICE integrated assessment model. Quantifies that AI development is net-polluting under current mitigation scenarios, adding 0.1–0.8°C to 2100 warming depending on AI trajectory.

**Method:**
Extended DICE model incorporating three AI impact channels: direct emissions from AI infrastructure/computation, productivity gains affecting economic output, and resulting climate damages. Distinguishes between ICT-like (low-impact) and Industrial Revolution-like (high-impact) AI scenarios calibrated to empirical evidence.

**Data:**
Calibrated to "best available evidence" on AI's emissions footprint, economic productivity effects, and climate damage functions; specific datasets not detailed in abstract but grounded in DICE model literature.

**Climate Relevance:**
Quantifies AI's contribution to anthropogenic warming and demonstrates that climate mitigation investments are complementary to—not competitive with—AI development. Shows climate costs offset 20–25% of AI's economic gains under low-abatement scenarios.

**Implications:**
Policymakers should coordinate AI investment with aggressive emissions abatement rather than treating them as trade-offs. Meeting 2°C targets requires higher optimal AI investment rates, suggesting climate policy enables rather than constrains AI development.

**Limitations:**
Abstract does not specify uncertainty ranges, sensitivity analyses, or model assumptions; calibration methodology and data sources require full paper review.

**TL;DR:**
AI accelerates warming by 0.1–0.8°C by 2100, but climate mitigation and AI investment are complementary policy objectives, not competing ones.

# Research Summary: AI Weather Models and Physical Fidelity

**Main Contribution:**
Identifies a fundamental trade-off in AI weather prediction (AIWP) models: their forecast accuracy stems from implicit learning of subgrid-scale effects, but this comes at the cost of violating physical principles (thermodynamic arrow of time, butterfly effect) due to inevitable data coarse-graining during training.

**Method:**
Comparative analysis across a hierarchy of systems (reanalysis data, general circulation models, Lorenz system) examining both forward forecasting and backward backcasting. Models tested include Pangu-Weather; physical fidelity assessed by checking for thermodynamic consistency and sensitivity to initial conditions (butterfly effect).

**Data:**
Observation-based reanalysis, general circulation model outputs, and the multi-scale Lorenz system as a controlled testbed for chaos dynamics.

**Climate Relevance:**
Directly addresses the reliability of emerging AI weather models now operationally deployed for forecasting. Raises critical questions about whether AIWP models can be trusted for long-term climate emulation and seasonal-to-decadal predictions where physical consistency matters.

**Implications:**
AIWP models achieve skill by learning effective parameterizations of unresolved scales, but this "black box" approach may fail in regimes outside training data. Backcasting emerges as a diagnostic tool for assessing physical fidelity. Suggests need for hybrid physics-AI approaches and revised predictability frameworks.

**Limitations:**
Analysis primarily uses idealized systems (Lorenz); generalization to full Earth system complexity unclear. Trade-off between accuracy and physical fidelity may be fundamental, limiting applicability to long-range climate projections.

**TL;DR:**
AI weather models forecast accurately by implicitly learning subgrid physics, but sacrifice thermodynamic consistency and chaos sensitivity—a trade-off with implications for climate prediction reliability.

# Research Summary: Temperature Extremes and French Power Infrastructure

**Main Contribution:**
Quantifies extreme temperature statistics over continental France using Bayesian non-stationary extreme value analysis, with explicit uncertainty estimates. Provides high-resolution spatial projections (0.25° grid) of temperature extremes through 2099 under multiple climate scenarios, directly applicable to infrastructure vulnerability assessment.

**Method:**
- Non-stationary Generalised Extreme Value (GEV) distribution fitted in Bayesian framework
- Ensemble approach combining 28 CMIP6 climate models with E-OBS observational data
- Uncertainty quantification for all parameter estimates
- Spatial interpolation enabling location-specific extreme value estimates

**Data:**
- CMIP6 multi-model ensemble (28 models)
- E-OBS observational temperature records
- Three emission scenarios (SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0, SSP5-8.5)
- Historical + future periods (1850–2099)

**Climate Relevance:**
Addresses compound climate risks: intensifying heat extremes under anthropogenic warming with direct consequences for critical infrastructure. Demonstrates spatial heterogeneity of temperature extremes across French regions, essential for regional adaptation planning.

**Implications:**
- Occitanie region potentially reaches 57°C by 2080 (high-emission scenario)
- Current national adaptation framework (TRACC) may underestimate extremes by up to +8°C
- Enables targeted vulnerability assessments for five electrical grid locations
- Supports infrastructure design and climate adaptation policy

**Limitations:**
Model uncertainty inherent to CMIP6 ensemble; GEV assumptions may not capture unprecedented compound events; 0.25° resolution may obscure local topographic effects on temperature extremes.

**TL;DR:**
Bayesian extreme value analysis of CMIP6 ensemble data projects potentially severe temperature extremes (57°C in southern France by 2080) with quantified uncertainties, revealing significant gaps in current national climate adaptation frameworks for electrical infrastructure.
